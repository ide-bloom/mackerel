# Milestone 2 — Advection

**Status:** done · **Commit:** see `git log --oneline -- docs/milestones/M2.md`

## Plan (written before coding)

* **Governing equation.** Advection is `dφ/dt = 0` along the flow, i.e.
  `∂φ/∂t + (u·∇)φ = 0`, applied to velocity (the non-linear term `(u·∇)u`) and to scalars
  (dye, temperature).
* **Discretisation.** Semi-Lagrangian: backtrace `x_d = x − ∫u dt` with RK2 (midpoint) or
  RK3, then bilinear interpolation from padded arrays. MacCormack and BFECC add a backward
  sweep to estimate and subtract the interpolation error, with a local min/max limiter.
* **Stability.** None required: semi-Lagrangian schemes are unconditionally stable in the
  von-Neumann sense because no spatial derivative of the advected quantity is evaluated.
  Accuracy degrades as the CFL number `C = dt·max(|u|+|v|)/h` grows past ~1, so the solver
  keeps `C ≤ 1` (`diagnostics.max_cfl`).
* **Correctness tests.** Exact translation at constant velocity; RK2 vs RK3 agreement on a
  linear field; rotation invariance; measured conservation and monotonicity; limiter on/off
  behaviour; wall behaviour of the backtrace.

## (b) The equation or algorithm each piece implements

**Backtrace (RK2).** `x_m = x − ½dt·u(x)`, `x_d = x − dt·u(x_m)`; RK3 is the standard
three-stage SSP Runge–Kutta.

**Semi-Lagrangian.** `φ_new(x) = φ_old(x_d)`, bilinear interpolation:
`φ = (1−tx)(1−ty)φ00 + tx(1−ty)φ10 + (1−tx)ty·φ01 + tx·ty·φ11`.

**MacCormack.** `fwd = SL(φ)`, `back = SL†(fwd)` where `SL†` traces *forward* in time
(velocity reversed) from the grid points, then `φ̃ = fwd − ½(back − φ)`. The correction is
clamped to `[min, max]` of the 3×3 neighbourhood of `φ`.

**BFECC.** `err = ½(back − φ)`, corrected field `φ − err`, then one more forward sweep.

**Limiter.** Per cell, `clip(φ̃, min₃ₓ₃(φ), max₃ₓ₃(φ))`, with the neighbourhood wrapped on
periodic axes.

## (c) Three to six sentences on the numerical tradeoffs

Bilinear interpolation is the source of numerical diffusion in a semi-Lagrangian scheme, and
the measurement confirms the textbook picture: one full revolution of a Gaussian blob on
128² leaves an RMS error of 5.2e-2, with the blob visibly widened and its peak dropped from
1.0 to about 0.6, and the error falls only at slope 0.41 as the grid is refined — much worse
than second order, because the number of interpolation steps grows as the grid is refined, so
per-step `O(h²)` errors accumulate into `O(h)` globals (the measured slope is even below 1
because the blob is only ~2 cells wide at the coarsest resolution, where the "error" stops
growing). MacCormack and BFECC recover most of that accuracy (2.5e-2 and 1.0e-2 at 128²,
slopes 1.56 and 2.05) at the cost of one or two extra interpolation sweeps per step, i.e.
2–3× the arithmetic, and — crucially — at the cost of conservation: after 100 steps of
periodic translation the semi-Lagrangian scheme conserves mass to 1e-14 while MacCormack
drifts by +1.0% and BFECC by −1.6%. The limiter is the reason: it is a nonlinear, per-cell
clamp, and a clamp is not a conservative operator; without it the same top-hat experiment
explodes to values of ±2.8e9, so the limiter is not optional. That is the real trade being
made in this milestone — accuracy of the transported shape versus exact conservation of its
integral — and both sides are measured above rather than assumed.

## Bugs found and fixed during this milestone (recorded because they changed the results)

1. **MacCormack/BFECC were non-convergent.** The first implementation traced the backward
   sweep from the *departure points*. That does not invert the forward map
   (`x → x − dt·u(x)` is not a rigid shift), so the error estimate was noise: a sine wave
   translated by one cell gained a 0.76 spurious heap in its first step. Fixed by tracing
   forward in time from the grid points (treat the padded velocity as a frozen field, insert
   `−dt`). After the fix the same test is exact to 2.5e-11 in all three schemes.
2. **The limiter leaked at periodic seams.** `np.pad(mode="edge")` clamped the neighbourhood
   at the seam, so values arriving from the far side of the domain were clipped one cell per
   step (mass −10% over 100 steps). Fixed by wrapping on periodic axes.
3. **Limiting the *destination* for BFECC destroyed the field in one step** (explosion to
   1e7) because the second forward sweep moves the departure points away from the region
   whose bounds are known. BFECC now limits the *corrected field* before the final sweep,
   which is the standard formulation, and the result is monotone.

## Assumptions / deviations recorded in this milestone

* **D9** The limiter is a local 3×3 min/max clamp, not the strict
  flux-corrected-transport (FCT/Zalesak) limiter. Consequence, measured above: corrected
  schemes are not conservative. Recorded rather than hidden behind an unenforced
  conservation claim.
* **D10** "Backward sweep" is implemented as a forward-in-time trace on the frozen velocity
  field. That makes the forward/backward pair an exact inverse for uniform flow (a
  translatable test), which the departure-point version is not.
* **D11** The rotating-blob experiment uses no-slip walls, so the flow field is rigid
  rotation inside the box; the blob's trajectory never touches a wall.
* **D12** Slope fits for the semi-Lagrangian scheme are reported as measured (0.41). They are
  *not* first order either, because the blob is under-resolved at the coarsest grid; the fit
  is a description of this experiment, not a claim about the scheme's asymptotic order.

## Questions you should be able to answer

1. Why does semi-Lagrangian advection *lose* peak height on a blob even though it solves the
   advection equation exactly along each trajectory?
2. Why is the naive MacCormack implementation (backward sweep from the departure points)
   wrong, and what property of the map `x → x − dt·u(x)` makes the corrected version an exact
   inverse only for uniform flow?
3. The limited MacCormack scheme drifts in total mass by ~1% while the semi-Lagrangian scheme
   conserves to 1e-14. Which operation causes the drift, and why can't you fix it by
   loosening the limiter (what did the unlimited run do)?
4. If the flow were a pure shear (`u = y`), would the bilinear limiter still suppress the
   overshoot that a high-order scheme produces?
