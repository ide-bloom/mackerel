# Milestone 7 — PIC/FLIP free-surface water

## Plan and acceptance criteria

Implement a particle-in-cell / fluid-implicit-particle liquid on the existing staggered MAC
grid, with a genuine atmospheric free-surface pressure condition. Validate an instantaneous
2-D dam break against the Martin–Moyce (1952) front series, provide splash and sloshing demos,
and measure an optional Numba transfer kernel against the pure-NumPy reference. Save raw time
series and particle data as well as figures/GIFs, and report errors rather than fitting the
simulation to the reference.

The acceptance checks are: finite particles and velocities, no particle loss in closed demos,
small liquid divergence after projection, a measured dam-break error table, reproducible demo
artifacts, NumPy/Numba equivalence checks, and a clean full test/lint/type pass.

## What was built

* `src/mackerel/flip.py`: structure-of-arrays particles, deterministic jittered seeding,
  bilinear MAC-face transfer, PIC/FLIP velocity updates, gravity, CFL-limited adaptive steps,
  closed-wall particle collisions, and nominal particle-volume diagnostics.
* `src/mackerel/project.py`: `free_surface=True` pressure systems. Liquid–air faces use an
  exterior `p=0` Dirichlet contribution; this is deliberately different from the zero-normal
  pressure gradient at an immersed solid. Only faces touching liquid are pressure-corrected.
* `src/mackerel/flip_numba.py`: optional, lazily compiled Numba transfer kernel. The default
  backend remains pure NumPy and Numba is not a base dependency.
* `validation/flip.py`: dam-break PIC and FLIP drivers, splash and tilted-water sloshing
  scenes, raw CSV/NPZ output, PNG figures and GIF animations.
* `validation/performance.py`: warmed-up NumPy/Numba transfer and complete-step benchmarks,
  including equality checks and separate JIT compilation timing.
* `validation/data/martin_moyce_1952.csv`: 14 `(tau,delta)` reference points with citation,
  digitisation source, normalization and gate-delay caveat.
* `mackerel run dam_break`, `mackerel run splash`, and `mackerel run sloshing` CLI scenes.
* `tests/test_flip.py`: interpolation, atmospheric-pressure, volume, short-run, final-time,
  and optional Numba regressions.

## Equations and algorithm

For each particle `p`, bilinear weights `w_{p,f}` transfer velocity to a MAC face `f`:

`u_f = sum_p(w_{p,f} u_p) / sum_p(w_{p,f})`.

The grid velocity is saved, gravity is added explicitly (`v <- v - g dt` on weighted `v`
faces), and the liquid pressure projection is solved. If `alpha` is the PIC fraction, the
particle update is

`u_p_new = alpha*u_grid_new + (1-alpha)*(u_p_old + u_grid_new - u_grid_old)`.

Thus `alpha=1` is pure PIC and `alpha=.05` is the reported 95%-FLIP blend. Particles are then
advected with their updated velocities. The default adaptive step is
`min(dt_max, .5*h/max|u_particle|)`; an explicitly supplied `dt` is respected and the final
step is clamped by the drivers to the requested end time.

In a liquid cell, `-laplacian(p) = -div(u*)/dt`. A liquid–air face contributes a diagonal
Dirichlet term with pressure zero on the air side. Air cells have no physical pressure
unknown and are returned as exactly `p=0`; air–air faces do not receive a pressure correction.
Domain walls still impose their normal velocity condition. Interpolation indices and their
fractional weights are clamped together at domain edges, preventing negative weights for
particles within half a cell of a wall.

## Reproduction

From the repository root:

```bash
python -m validation.flip                 # dam break, splash, sloshing -> outputs/m7
python -m validation.performance          # optional Numba benchmark -> outputs/m7
mackerel run dam_break --n 48 --steps 200
mackerel run splash --n 40 --steps 200
mackerel run sloshing --n 40 --steps 200
python -m pytest -q
ruff check .
mypy
```

The full validation driver runs both methods on a 48x36 grid, with a `1 x .75` domain,
initial column `a=.25` wide and `2a=.5` high, `g=9.81`, `dt_max=.0005`, and 669 steps to
`tau=2.96`. The front diagnostic is the rightmost particle plus `h/2`, divided by `a`;
`tau=t*sqrt(2g/a)`. No gate-opening delay is added.

## Measured dam-break results

The reference is the documented 14-point Martin–Moyce digitisation. Errors are computed by
interpolating each simulation's complete time series to the reference `tau` values. The
initial half-cell front convention gives `delta=1.024931` at `tau=0`; this discretisation
offset is included in the metrics rather than removed.

| quantity | 95% FLIP (`alpha=.05`) | pure PIC (`alpha=1`) |
|---|---:|---:|
| reference points | 14 | 14 |
| front RMSE | **0.072551** | **0.622552** |
| maximum absolute front error | **0.113702** | **1.144789** |
| mean relative front error, excluding `tau=0` | **3.3447%** | **19.5755%** |
| maximum liquid divergence during run | `3.3662e-7` | `9.0968e-8` |
| final liquid divergence | `2.9167e-8` | `7.3712e-9` |
| nominal volume relative error | `0` | `0` |
| wall time in this sandbox | `3.246 s` | `3.208 s` |

FLIP's largest positive deviation is `+0.113702` at `tau=2.20` (predicted `2.673702`,
reference `2.56`). PIC increasingly lags the experimental front, ending at `2.525211` versus
`3.67` at `tau=2.96`. These are measured outcomes of the two updates, not a claim that one
universally converges to the experiment. The reference is a secondary digitisation, and the
particle-front definition is not identical to a laboratory interface threshold.

## NumPy versus Numba

Numba `0.68.1` and NumPy `2.4.6` were used on the sandbox's Linux x86-64 environment. JIT
compilation took `.19285 s` and was excluded from steady-state timings. Transfer timings are
medians of seven runs of the `u`-face transfer; both `u` and `v` outputs were checked first.

| grid | particles | NumPy (ms) | Numba (ms) | speedup | max checked difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 32x32 | 4,096 | `.452831` | `.036094` | `12.55x` | `2.66e-15` |
| 64x64 | 16,384 | `2.008120` | `.161267` | `12.45x` | `1.78e-15` |
| 128x128 | 65,536 | `10.200012` | `.736513` | `13.85x` | `2.66e-15` |

A complete 32x24 water run of 40 steps (512 particles, including the same pressure solve)
took `.096852 s` with NumPy and `.090994 s` with Numba: only `1.064x` faster. The largest
particle-component discrepancy was `3.053e-16`. The transfer kernel is therefore substantially
faster in isolation, but pressure projection dominates this small full solve; the transfer
speedup must not be advertised as a whole-solver speedup. Without the optional dependency,
the benchmark reports that no accelerated timing is available and the NumPy solver still works.

## Numerical limitations and tradeoffs

* This is a 2-D, inviscid particle liquid with no surface tension, contact-angle model, air
  momentum, turbulence model, or body-fitted free surface. Particle occupancy gives a
  cell-scale interface and can produce FLIP noise or PIC numerical diffusion.
* No particle reseeding or conservative volume-of-fluid remapping is performed. Closed-wall
  collisions clamp positions and zero the outward normal component. Exact particle-count /
  nominal-volume conservation is therefore a property of the representation, not an
  independent proof of conservative interface transport.
* Divergence is measured on liquid cells after each pressure solve; it is not zero to machine
  precision because the relative CG tolerance is finite and the free-surface mask changes as
  particles move. The maximum values above are retained in the raw CSV files.
* The dam-break comparison is a single resolution and a single front convention, not a
  grid-convergence or experimental-uncertainty study. The half-cell offset, staircase
  occupancy, lack of viscosity, and secondary digitisation all affect the deviation.
* Numba accelerates only particle transfer. It is lazily imported, serial (to avoid racing
  shared-face accumulations), and checked against NumPy before timing.

## Questions for the reader

1. Why does a liquid–air face add a pressure diagonal term while a liquid–solid face removes
   its coupling?
2. What is the difference between the PIC and FLIP particle velocity updates, and why does
   pure PIC lag the dam-break front here?
3. Why must interpolation fractions be clamped along with indices at a wall?
4. Why is exact particle-count conservation weaker than conservative free-surface volume
   transport?
5. Why can a 13x transfer speedup produce only a 1.06x complete-step speedup?
